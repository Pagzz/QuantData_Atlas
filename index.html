01

Summary

Spreads carry most of the risk

Five option structures with the maximum loss known at entry. The overbought call spread won 90% of the time over 33 years; the rich-premium iron condor won 91%. Each beats the same structure opened on every day.

Short-term: hours to days

Buying sharp drops where selling has historically run out. Win rates of 68–85% on SPY and QQQ; the same rules stayed profitable on the other 12 ETFs, with smaller edges.

Longer-term: weeks to months

A 40-day index breakout that wins 39% of the time but whose winners are 3× its losers, and sector-ETF rotation that returned +10.2% a year against +7.7% for the S&P 500 ETF.

Most ideas fail

Key price levels, calendar effects, momentum rotation and many intraday patterns were rejected. Section 09 lists them.

02

Method

A signal goes live only after passing all six checks. Most candidates fail at step 4 or 5.

  1. Point-in-time dataCompleted bars only; volatility filters use the prior day's VIX.
  2. The real exit, with costsTrades use the live exit rule, not a fixed holding period. Costs: 3 bp per ETF round trip, $0.01 per option leg.
  3. Both halvesProfitable in each half of the test period. Daily rules are found on 1993–2009 and must hold on 2010–2026.
  4. Unseen marketsUnchanged on 12 other ETFs (nine S&P 500 Select Sector SPDRs, plus the Dow, Russell 2000 and semiconductors) and, for daily rules, the 100 largest U.S. stocks.
  5. Beat a baselineBetter than random entry days with the same exit, or the same spread opened every day.
  6. A plateau, not a spikeNearby settings and bar sizes must also work.

Options are priced with Black-Scholes, using the prior day's VIX (SPY) or VXN (QQQ) and a standard index skew. Modeled prices, not broker fills.

Conventions

  • Close decisions at 3:45 pm. Signals that need the close use the 3:45 pm price; this matched the real close on 85–92% of signals.
  • Exchange-clock bars. Multi-hour bars are cut on the exchange clock, after a daylight-saving drift was found and fixed.
  • Price adjustments. Adjusted prices for returns; unadjusted prices for live comparisons with past highs, since a dividend-adjusted high sits below the traded price.
  • Vendor reconciliation. Schwab reports 15–25% less volume than the consolidated tape, so live volume is rescaled on overlapping bars.
  • Known bias. The stock universe is today's 100 largest companies (survivorship), so stocks are used only as a pass/fail check.

Production

A Python service (FastAPI, pandas, NumPy) on Render runs each signal on its bar clock, merges real-time and historical data, tracks open positions and posts entries and exits. Before launch, each signal's production code is replayed over history and must reproduce the research results.

09

What failed

  • Key price levelsNo better than randomly placed placebo levels.
  • Breakouts on sector ETFs and stocksMatched random entries; the edge exists only on the broad indexes.
  • Breakdowns, squeezes, opening-range, gap-and-goNone held out of sample.
  • 4-hour divergence, daily reversalFailed the ETF check, or lost money 1999–2026.
  • Put spreads on weak daysProfitable since 2016, but lost money 1993–2015.
  • Momentum and relative-strength rotationAt or below buy-and-hold after risk.
  • Overnight-return momentumMostly market exposure; hedged, it lost about 2% a year.
  • Calendar, holiday and lunar effectsNone passed a permutation test.

11

Limitations

  • Modeled option prices. Real bid-ask spreads, especially on short-dated contracts, would reduce returns.
  • Multiple testing. Thousands of rules were screened; the checks above guard against this, but reported numbers are likely somewhat optimistic.
  • Survivorship. The stock universe is today's largest companies; point-in-time index membership would fix this.
  • Regimes. Several signals need high volatility and can go quiet for weeks.
  • Overlap. Many buys fire in the same selloffs; results are per signal, not a portfolio.

Glossary

Profit factor
Gross profits divided by gross losses. 1.0 breaks even; above 2 is strong.
R
Profit in units of the risk taken. Risking 2% to make 6% is 3R.
Return on risk
For spreads: profit divided by the spread's maximum loss.
Max drawdown
Largest peak-to-trough fall, with the full account in each trade.
Implied vs realized volatility
The movement option prices expect versus the movement that happened.
