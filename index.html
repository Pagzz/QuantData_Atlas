01

Summary

The research asks one question over and over: after a specific, measurable market condition, did the market behave differently from a random day — and does that hold up outside the data it was found in? The answers are grouped by how risk is taken and by holding period.

Defined-risk spreads carry most of the risk

Five option structures, each with its maximum loss known at entry. Selling call spreads after overbought closes won 90% of the time from 1993 to 2026. A rich-premium iron condor won 91%. A stop at the spread's midpoint removed every full loss without lowering the return. Every structure beats the same structure opened on every day.

Short-term: selling exhaustion, hours to days

Most short-term signals buy a sharp intraday or daily drop at the point where selling has historically faded. Win rates run 68–85%. The same rules, unchanged, stayed profitable on 12 other index and sector ETFs (profit factors 1.2–1.9), with smaller edges than on the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100.

Longer-term: trends and sector rotation, weeks to months

A sector-ETF rotation model returned +10.2% a year against +7.7% for the S&P 500 ETF from 2000 to 2024, with a shallower drawdown (−36% against −55%). A 40-day breakout captures multi-week index trends: it wins 39% of the time, but winners average about 3× the losers.

Knowing where an edge stops

The breakout beat random entries on the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 ETFs in every era since 1993, but not on sector ETFs or single stocks. It is traded only where it works. Short-term dip-buying, by contrast, carried across sector ETFs.

Most ideas fail, and the failures are recorded

Key price levels, calendar effects, momentum rotation, overnight momentum and several intraday patterns were tested and rejected. Section 10 lists them with the reason each failed.

02

Method: the validation ladder

A signal goes live only after it clears every step. Most candidates fail at step 4 or 5.

  1. Point-in-time dataSignals use completed bars only. Volatility filters use the prior day's VIX. Signals decided "at the close" are computed at 3:45 pm and checked against the real close: the estimate agreed 85–92% of the time and traded as well.
  2. The real exit, with costsEach trade is simulated with the exit rule the live alert uses (trailing stop, return to the average, stop-loss, time limit), not a fixed holding period. Costs are 3 bp per round trip for ETFs, 5 bp for stocks and $0.01 per option leg.
  3. Split-sample stabilityResults must be positive in both halves of the test window. Daily rules are found on 1993–2009 and then must hold on 2010–2026 unchanged.
  4. Markets it never sawThe same rule, with no re-tuning, is run on 12 other ETFs: nine of the S&P 500 Select Sector SPDRs (GICS sector funds), plus the Dow, Russell 2000 and semiconductor ETFs. Daily rules are also run on the 100 largest U.S. stocks.
  5. Beat a naive baselineEach signal must beat random entry days with the same exit, or the same option spread opened every day. The baseline removes the market's upward drift and the premium options normally carry.
  6. A plateau, not a spikeNearby settings and nearby bar sizes (5-minute to 4-hour) must also work. An edge that only appears at one exact setting is treated as noise.

Option returns come from a Black-Scholes model. Implied volatility is the prior day's VIX (SPY) or VXN (QQQ), with a standard index skew. Same-day options are calibrated so that buying an at-the-money straddle every day roughly breaks even, as it does in practice. These are modeled prices, not broker fills.

Point-in-time conventions

  • Completed bars only. A signal on a bar is evaluated after the bar closes. Higher-timeframe context comes from the last bar completed by that moment.
  • Lagged volatility. Volatility filters and option prices use the prior day's VIX or VXN close.
  • Wall-clock bar construction. Multi-hour bars are cut on the exchange clock. This fixed a daylight-saving drift that had shifted 4-hour bars by an hour.
  • Close-based signals estimated at 3:45 pm. The live system decides at 3:45 pm. The estimate was checked against the real close: it matched 85–92% of signals and produced the same results.
  • Price adjustments. Split- and dividend-adjusted history is used for research returns. Live rules that compare today's price with past levels, such as a 40-day high, use unadjusted prices, because a dividend-adjusted high sits below the price actually traded. When two vendors adjusted the same history differently, signals were rebuilt from a single source rather than mixed.
  • Vendor reconciliation. Real-time Schwab bars report 15–25% less volume than the consolidated-tape history. Live volume is rescaled on the overlapping bars, so volume-based rules keep the meaning they were tested with.
  • Known biases stated. The stock universe is today's 100 largest companies (survivorship). It is used only as a pass/fail check, never to tune a rule.

Production system

The signals run as a Python service: FastAPI, pandas and NumPy, deployed on Render. A scheduler fires each timeframe on the bar clock, merges Schwab real-time bars into Alpaca history, keeps position state on persistent storage, removes duplicate alerts, and posts entries and exits to subscribers. Before any signal goes live, its production code is replayed over years of history and must reproduce the research results: the latest additions matched 90% of research signals, with the same win rates. Monitoring uses the service's health endpoint and its logs.

Defined-risk spreads

The main way these ideas take risk. Each structure has a capped loss, a fixed horizon and a tested exit. Each is measured against the same structure opened on every day, so the result shows what the signal adds beyond the premium options normally carry. Returns are a percentage of the capital at risk.

Why spreads. The maximum loss is known before the trade, so positions are sized by risk rather than by price. Index options usually price in more movement than the market delivers, so a spread sold on an ordinary day already earns a small premium in the model. The signals below decide when that premium is richest, and which side to sell.

Cutting the full losses: which stop works?

A credit spread's worst case is losing its whole width. Seven exit rules were replayed on 5-minute bars, 2016–2026. For the overbought call spread the winner is to buy it back once price trades through the midpoint between the two strikes. For the iron condor it is a stop at either short strike.

The common "stop at 2× the credit" rule turned a profitable setup into a losing one. Next-day spreads swing too much intraday, so it stops out trades that would have expired worthless. A stop at the short strike cut losses too, but gave up more return than the midpoint. Only an overnight gap through both strikes can still cause a large loss. The live alert now shows the midpoint stop level.

Sizing and execution

  • Size by the maximum loss. Risk per spread is the width minus the credit. About one spread in ten loses, so each is sized so a large loss is acceptable.
  • Minimum credit. The condor is taken only if the real credit is at least 85% of the model price, because the edge shrinks when options trade cheap.
  • Residual risk is the overnight gap. Stops cannot act while the market is closed. A gap through both strikes can still cost most of the risk.
  • Beyond SPY and QQQ. Spread results are modeled on the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 ETFs, which have 33 years of implied-volatility history (VIX, VXN). The signals that trigger them were validated on the sector ETFs. Applying the same structures to liquid sector-ETF options such as XLF, XLE or XLK is the natural next step; it needs each fund's own volatility history.

Rejected: a put credit spread after a washout, which barely beat its every-day version; iron condors in high-VIX markets, with 25–38% full losses; and the "2× credit" stop.

Longer-term ideas: weeks to months

Two holding periods beyond the short-term book: multi-week index trends, and sector ETF rotation measured in months.

Trend Breakout · weeks

A close at the highest level in 40 trading days: buy with a stop below, and hold until the price closes under its 20-day low. It is built to capture large moves, winning less than half the time with winners about three times the size of losers.

Premium stop-losses on bought options made results worse; risk is controlled through position size and strike choice.

What failed

A research log of ideas that were tested and rejected, and why. Ideas that fail are a normal part of the process.

  • Key price levels & volume profilePrice action around major daily levels was no better than around randomly placed placebo levels.
  • Breakouts on sector ETFs and single stocksThe 40-day breakout matched random entries on 12 ETFs and 100 stocks. Its edge is specific to the broad indexes.
  • Short breakdowns, squeezes, opening-range and gap-and-goNo version produced the targeted 50/50, 3–5× payoff profile out of sample.
  • 4-hour divergence and daily reversal buysStrong on SPY/QQQ but failed the 12-ETF check (4-hour), or lost money 1999–2026 (daily).
  • Put spreads on weak daysProfitable after 2016 but lost money from 1993 to 2015, so they were rejected despite the attractive recent record.
  • "2× credit" spread stopTurned a profit factor of 2.4 into 0.9 on next-day spreads, because intraday swings stop out eventual winners.
  • Sector momentum & relative-strength rotationDual momentum, relative-strength breakouts and relative-value rotation all came in at or below buy-and-hold on a risk-adjusted basis; the market-neutral version earned about zero.
  • Overnight-return momentumIt looked strong but was mostly market exposure. Hedged, it lost about 2% a year.
  • Calendar, holiday and lunar effectsNone passed a permutation test (Appendix).
  • Six weaker live alerts retiredIn September 2026 the six weakest or most redundant signals were cut to keep the alert set small and high quality.

Limitations

  • Modeled option prices. Options and spreads are priced from index volatility with a standard skew, not historical quotes. Real bid-ask spreads, especially on short-dated contracts, would reduce returns.
  • Multiple testing. Thousands of rules were screened. Split samples, untouched ETFs, random baselines and parameter plateaus guard against this, but the reported numbers for the chosen rules are still likely somewhat optimistic.
  • Survivorship. The stock universe is today's 100 largest companies, which flatters results on stocks. Stocks are only used as a pass/fail check. The proper fix is point-in-time index membership, meaning the constituents as they stood on each date, including deletions.
  • Regime dependence. Several signals are active only in high-volatility markets and can go quiet for weeks. About 38% of days have any alert.
  • Correlation between signals. Many buys fire in the same selloffs. Results are per signal, not a combined portfolio.
  • Spreads on sector ETFs not yet modeled. The sector ETFs lack a long implied-volatility history, so spread results are shown for the index ETFs only.
  • Intraday history starts in 2016. Intraday signals have ten years of data; daily ones have 33.

Correlations worth knowing

  • Fear spikes mean-revert. After SPY falls more than the options market implied for the day, the next five days have averaged +0.45%, about double the normal +0.23%.
  • Broad selloffs bounce better than narrow ones. Dip-buys taken when most of the largest stocks were below their own average did markedly better.
  • Bond yields lead gold miners by about three months. Rising 10-year yields were followed by weaker gold miners 10–13 weeks later (correlation −0.17).
  • The Fed's balance sheet tracked stocks, then stopped. The link with six-month SPY returns (+0.25) disappeared out of sample; it was mostly a quantitative-easing-era effect.

Glossary

Win rate
Share of trades that made money after costs.
Profit factor
Gross profits divided by gross losses. 1.0 breaks even; above 2 is strong.
Per trade
Average return per trade after costs, winners and losers together.
R
Profit measured in units of the risk taken. A trade risking 2% that gains 6% made 3R.
Return on risk
For spreads: profit divided by the maximum possible loss of the spread.
Max drawdown
Largest peak-to-trough fall of the equity curve, assuming the whole account in each trade in sequence.
In / at / out of the money
A call's strike below / at / above the current price. In-the-money options cost more but move more like the shares.
Implied vs realized volatility
The movement option prices expect versus the movement that actually occurred recently.
Baseline
The same trade taken on random or ordinary days. A signal must beat it.
